How much was this particular investment?

On printed pp4697–4698 (PDF pp117–118), Peterson identifies Dynasty Financial II LLC as the legal investing entity on RDV's behalf. Questioned about admitted Exhibit 4845, she confirms $99,999,984 as the investment amount and 31 October 2014 as its approximate date. This supports describing this case as an approximately $100m investment; it is not a claim about every DeVos family member's participation. We have inspected the transcript, not the standalone transaction document or bank statement. The amount does not establish what Theranos spent the proceeds on.

Exhibit 2166: the investor's memo, not a Theranos pitch deck

On printed pp4691–4693 (PDF pp111–113), Peterson identifies Government Exhibit 2166 as the RDV approval document she prepared after the 14 October 2014 meeting for the investment committee. What mattered: proprietary equipment and portability

Printed pp4693–4697 (PDF pp113–117) record Peterson saying that manufacture of the analyser equipment was relevant to underwriting. A memo passage describes a small analyser station that could be placed in an operating room, military evacuation helicopter, ship or refugee camp; she attributes that information to Holmes and Balwani and calls portability critical to her understanding. This is a claimed capability with potential deployment locations, not evidence of actual military use. She also says she was never told before investing that third-party machines did some or most testing, or that the TSPU ran no more than twelve assays. Those are her sworn recollections, not recordings of the meeting or proof that every investor heard the same claims.

900 locations in 2015 was a plan, not 900 operating sites

Printed pp4694–4695 (PDF pp114–115) quote the memo's planned 900 Walgreens wellness centres in five metropolitan markets the following year. Peterson attributes it to the Palo Alto conversation and denies being told locations were being scaled back. Treat the number as a forecast. Separately, the amended appellate opinion pp14–15 recounts Walgreens testimony that the partnership was contracting while expansion was being promoted. A future rollout target and the actual state of a partnership are different propositions: failure to achieve the former alone does not establish that the latter was honestly described.

Pharmaceutical credibility was influential—but disputed

Printed pp4693–4694 (PDF pp113–114) record a memo assertion of comprehensive validation by ten pharmaceutical companies. The spoken date is corrected from 2015 to 2005; this remains an attributed assertion, not an established validation history. Peterson says validation, including Pfizer, mattered to underwriting. The court overruled a Rule 602 objection: that kept the answers in evidence, it did not certify scientific performance. The amended appellate opinion p15 recounts Holmes admitting she added pharmaceutical logos to reports, and company representatives denying independent validation or permission to use their logos. A report carrying a familiar logo is not automatically an independently authored validation study.

The defence counterpoint: what could she actually recall?

On printed pp4711–4712 (PDF pp131–132), Peterson could not recall the Pfizer study's kind, location, drug or particular tests. She nevertheless described reading the reports at the time and taking them as support for analyser accuracy; she rejected the suggestion that she remembered only the logo. This limits how precisely her later testimony can reconstruct scientific diligence. It neither establishes that no diligence occurred nor supplies missing independent validation. Her stated investment impression and the underlying scientific evidence must be checked separately.

A demonstration needs an instrument-to-result trail

The amended appellate opinion recounts employee testimony that some VIP demonstrations ran a null protocol on the displayed Theranos device while samples were tested elsewhere on third-party equipment. This is the court's account of trial evidence, not our reconstruction of every visit. A visitor seeing a machine and receiving a result therefore does not, without a linked run record, establish that the displayed machine produced that result. This concerns represented present capability, not merely a future forecast.

The decisive test is dated capability, not whether innovation was possible

Read each representation in its own tense: what already operated, what was under development, and what financial outcome was forecast. Grossman's 2013 testimony and Peterson's 2014 testimony concern different encounters. What did the capability claims actually say?

SEC complaint paragraphs 53–60 allege present capability claims covering over 1,000 CPT codes, a solution for another 300, tests on one analyser and one consumable, and manufacture of all analysers despite third-party use. What this already clarifies

A financial forecast is explicitly about a future period. A claim that a proprietary instrument already performs a test is about present capability. An investment pitch can contain both, and uncertainty in the forecast does not automatically qualify the separate technical representation. Establish actual delivery and reliance before explaining why this investor invested.

Was it a future promise or an existing capability?

The following is our comparison of the records above, not a quotation from the film. A pitch can mix present claims, future plans and forecasts. They need different tests.

Separate the promise from the evidence needed to support it
RepresentationQuestion to testWhat the record adds
Proprietary analyser and portable stationWhich tests could the specified instrument reliably perform at the investment date, under which conditions?Peterson's memo testimony describes its importance. 900 Walgreens locations the following yearWas this a future plan, and was the existing partnership accurately described?Forecast and partnership evidence must be separated. Missing a target alone does not prove deception.

Provisional conclusion

This investor's account was not simply a bet that an invention might work one day. Peterson describes reliance on proprietary equipment, portability and purported existing pharmaceutical validation, alongside future financial and rollout forecasts. The memo testimony narrows why those propositions mattered, while cross-examination limits how precisely scientific diligence can be reconstructed. This remains one investor case, not every investor's motives or a complete transaction file. A matching dollar figure does not close the delivery gap.

What this lets us ask of the film

When a scene discusses what the invention “could do”, check whether it means demonstrated performance then, a proposed use, or future development. Compare like dates and devices.
