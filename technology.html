Technology / from sample to claim

What was the Theranos technology?

A small sample, a working assay, a patented system and a validated platform are different achievements.

Edited by the Theranos Film project · Briar assisted · Updated 21 November 2026. Editorial draft; not independently fact-checked. About this independent project · Corrections and review history.

The core idea—and the hard part

Offer useful blood testing from a very small sample, with an automated system that could make testing easier to access. That is a service promise plus an engineering programme, not a single discovery. The hard part is reliably delivering every advertised test, on the stated sample, in the stated workflow.

Five layers that must not be collapsed into “the invention”

From finger-prick to clinically usable information
LayerWhat must workWhat could invalidate the claim
1. CollectionObtain sufficient, representative material in a suitable container.Clotting, cell damage, contamination, tissue-fluid admixture, or an insufficient sample.
2. PreparationTransport, separate, mix or dilute the sample as required by the assay.Timing, temperature, retained volume, dilution uncertainty and unreported manual steps.
3. MeasurementUse an appropriate detection method for each intended analyte.Interference, inadequate signal, calibration errors or the wrong measurement range.
4. Instrument and workflowIntegrate modules and controls with repeatable operation and realistic throughput.Carryover, failed runs, selected demonstrations, undisclosed off-device processing or third-party instruments.
5. Report and intended useTranslate validated measurements into an accurate, usable report within the claimed scope.Misleading reference ranges, unsupported interpretation, missing limitations or unjustified test-menu claims.

This is a general engineering framework, not an assertion that each listed failure occurred in every Theranos device. Each mechanism needs device-, assay- and date-specific evidence.

What does “platform” actually mean?

A platform can be a common sample-handling system, a shared cartridge, several instruments in one enclosure, software linking modules, or a laboratory service. Those are different architectures. Cell counting, chemical concentration measurements, antibody-based assays and nucleic-acid tests require different measurement principles and often different preparation.

Integration is useful engineering, but it does not make every module clinically validated. A proposed later system must not be used as proof that an earlier product could already do the same things.

Why a tiny sample is not a universal shortcut

A usable sample has a budget: collection losses and retained material reduce what can be divided between tests, quality controls and repeat measurements. The relevant volume is the amount of original sample reaching each assay, not merely the amount collected.

Adding diluent increases liquid volume, not the amount of target material originally present. Whether a diluted sample is usable depends on the validated assay range, preparation uncertainty and detection performance. Some tests can work at small volumes; that does not establish that all tests can share one tiny sample under one protocol.

Miniaturising a reaction also leaves transport, mixing, contamination control, calibration, maintenance, waste handling and failed-run recovery to solve. The service has to work across realistic patients and samples, not just in a selected demonstration.

How to test the claim properly

  1. Freeze the claim: assay, device/version, sample type and volume, preparation, turnaround, throughput and intended use.
  2. Choose the comparison: an appropriate reference/comparator method and justified acceptance criteria, not simply a visually similar number.
  3. Measure performance: bias and repeatability, reportable range, interference, sample stability, invalid results and relevant sensitivity/specificity measures.
  4. Test realistic operation: different operators, sites, lots and clinically relevant samples; include failures and repeats rather than hiding them.
  5. Keep the scope: a validated assay is not a validated menu, and analytical agreement is not automatically clinical usefulness or regulatory permission.

What the available evidence does—and does not—show

The 2016 JCI comparison examined delivered testing services using 22 tests in 60 healthy adults. It provides a useful comparison within that design. It is not a complete instrument teardown, a study of every assay, or proof of which machine produced each individual result.

What was the intellectual property?

Start with a claim chart, not a patent count. For each relevant patent, record the priority date, inventors, owner at the relevant date, granted claims and any later assignment. Compare each claimed component with the actual product configuration and the capability pitched at the time.

A specification can describe embodiments and aspirations broader than the legal protection ultimately granted. Neither a patent grant nor an application is a clinical performance certificate. Trade secrets, software, know-how, contracts and regulatory evidence are separate assets and separate questions.

Was it “the wrong technology”?

We have not authenticated the engineer’s identity or the remembered exchange. It would be misleading to attribute a technical verdict to an unidentified speaker. The phrase could concern sample preparation, detection method, engineering architecture, development sequence or economics; those remain hypotheses.

The next useful step is the full passage and a precise comparison: what was being measured, why the chosen method was said to fail, which alternative was proposed, and what evidence supports that alternative. A later patent or successful method does not prove the earlier task was impossible; a promising experiment does not prove a commercial platform was ready.
